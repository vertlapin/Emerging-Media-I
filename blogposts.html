In Defense of the Poor Image (2009)

September 14, 2026 What interested me most about Hito Steyerl’s “In Defense of the Poor Image” was the repetition of “the poor image” at the beginning, which almost felt like a poem or prose. It made me imagine the poor image as a ghost or an itinerant image traveling from one place to another. I liked the idea that “the image is liberated from the vaults of cinema” and how poor images challenge the promises of digital technology and audiovisual capitalism. The idea of a “class society of images” was especially interesting to me, as well as the critique of the “cult of the mostly male genius.” This reminded me of how male-dominated the film industry is and made me think about how certain male filmmakers are treated as geniuses even when their movies can feel very male-centered. The reading also made me think about my own creative process, especially how I choose photographs for my portfolio. I usually choose images taken with my digital or film camera and leave out my iPhone photos because I think their lower resolution makes them less valuable, even though I love taking spontaneous pictures with my phone because it is always with me. Steyerl made me question why I automatically value one type of image over another. I also loved the reference to the film museum/archive in Sarajevo and the idea of archives finding another life as places where people can access films that are lost or forgotten. Instead of only preserving images, these spaces could help people see, share, and keep them alive. This made me think about “poor” or imperfect images not as failures, but as images that can travel, be shared, and belong to many people.

As We May Think (1945)

What I found most interesting about Vannevar Bush’s As We May Think is the idea that humans actually think. We remember things, collect things, and make connections between things that seem completely unrelated. Our mind works by association. I think about this a lot when I’m researching vintage dresses. I can start with a dress, then go into its construction, the fabric, the decade, a woman who might have worn it, an old photograph, a movie, a sound, a designer, another garment from another time period, and somehow end up back at the original dress. It’s basically a rabbit hole, but the path is what makes it interesting. Bush’s idea of the Memex really reminded me of this. A personal archive where you don’t just save information, but also your notes, memories, connections, and all your weird little rabbit holes. The trail you took becomes valuable too. It also made me think about algorithms now. Sometimes I’ll think about something I haven’t searched, Googled, or even said out loud, and then suddenly I see it on my feed. It’s a little creepy, but also interesting when you think about Bush imagining technology that could follow these associative connections. I also really liked what our professor mentioned about the people who later built the internet being influenced by this article. It’s kind of crazy to think that Bush was writing about these ideas in 1945. It’s not really about the destination, but about the journey (or both).

Chapters 1 & 2: The Society of the Spectacle by Guy Debord (1967)

September 29, 2026 What interested me most in Debord’s The Society of the Spectacle was his idea that we are increasingly experiencing life through images and representations instead of directly experiencing things ourselves. This made me think a lot about fashion and social media, especially Instagram. I sell vintage clothing, but I also photograph it, post it, style it, and think about how it will look on a screen. Sometimes I wonder if I am selling the actual garment or the image and feeling surrounding the garment. Something that is beautiful in person can look completely different in a photograph, and sometimes something that looks amazing online is actually disappointing in real life. The idea that the image can become more important than the thing itself felt very relevant to me. Debord describes the spectacle as more than just a collection of images, but as a way that our relationships become mediated through them, which made me think about how much of our lives now happens through screens. I also found the connection to “Welcome to Jankspace, Babes” really interesting because it feels like a very contemporary version of what Debord was talking about. The internet is now this strange, chaotic space filled with algorithms, AI slop, influencers, advertisements, trends, and endless images. I especially liked thinking about AI in relation to this because we are reaching a point where it can be difficult to tell what is real, what is manufactured, and what is made by a person. At the same time, I don’t think I completely dislike this spectacle. I actually love fashion, images, movies, and the weirdness of the internet. I think the part that makes me uncomfortable is when the representation becomes more important than the actual experience. It makes me wonder if we are becoming spectators of our own lives instead of actually living them.

Constituents of a theory of the media by Enzensberger (1970)

October 6, 2026 What I liked the most in Enzensberger’s “Constituents of a Theory of the Media” was the contradiction between how quickly media and technology evolve and how they are still controlled by capitalism and the people who own them. I thought about my dad, who was born in 1959, telling me about when television transitioned from black and white to color. I remember thinking how crazy it must have been to actually experience a change like that, but now we are seeing technology evolve faster than ever, to the point where things like AI can feel almost dystopian. It made me think about how media can become more powerful and accessible while still being socially controlled. I also liked the idea of media being reciprocal rather than just a transmitter sending information to a receiver. A socialist strategy for media should try to end the isolation of individual participants and create more communication between people. This immediately made me think about the comment sections on YouTube, TikTok, and Instagram. I always find it interesting to read people’s reactions because sometimes the comments completely change the way I see the original content. You find people who get it, people who see something you didn't, and people who completely kill the vibe lol. The comments become another layer of the media itself, and I think that kind of feedback is interesting because it turns media into more of a conversation instead of something that only moves in one direction.
