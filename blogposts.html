In Defense of the Poor Image (2009)

September 14, 2026 What interested me most about Hito Steyerl’s “In Defense of the Poor Image” was the repetition of “the poor image” at the beginning, which almost felt like a poem or prose. It made me imagine the poor image as a ghost or an itinerant image traveling from one place to another. I liked the idea that “the image is liberated from the vaults of cinema” and how poor images challenge the promises of digital technology and audiovisual capitalism. The idea of a “class society of images” was especially interesting to me, as well as the critique of the “cult of the mostly male genius.” This reminded me of how male-dominated the film industry is and made me think about how certain male filmmakers are treated as geniuses even when their movies can feel very male-centered. The reading also made me think about my own creative process, especially how I choose photographs for my portfolio. I usually choose images taken with my digital or film camera and leave out my iPhone photos because I think their lower resolution makes them less valuable, even though I love taking spontaneous pictures with my phone because it is always with me. Steyerl made me question why I automatically value one type of image over another. I also loved the reference to the film museum/archive in Sarajevo and the idea of archives finding another life as places where people can access films that are lost or forgotten. Instead of only preserving images, these spaces could help people see, share, and keep them alive. This made me think about “poor” or imperfect images not as failures, but as images that can travel, be shared, and belong to many people.

As We May Think (1945)

What I found most interesting about Vannevar Bush’s As We May Think is the idea that humans actually think. We remember things, collect things, and make connections between things that seem completely unrelated. Our mind works by association. I think about this a lot when I’m researching vintage dresses. I can start with a dress, then go into its construction, the fabric, the decade, a woman who might have worn it, an old photograph, a movie, a sound, a designer, another garment from another time period, and somehow end up back at the original dress. It’s basically a rabbit hole, but the path is what makes it interesting. Bush’s idea of the Memex really reminded me of this. A personal archive where you don’t just save information, but also your notes, memories, connections, and all your weird little rabbit holes. The trail you took becomes valuable too. It also made me think about algorithms now. Sometimes I’ll think about something I haven’t searched, Googled, or even said out loud, and then suddenly I see it on my feed. It’s a little creepy, but also interesting when you think about Bush imagining technology that could follow these associative connections. I also really liked what our professor mentioned about the people who later built the internet being influenced by this article. It’s kind of crazy to think that Bush was writing about these ideas in 1945. It’s not really about the destination, but about the journey (or both).

Chapters 1 & 2: The Society of the Spectacle by Guy Debord (1967)

September 29, 2026 What interested me most in Debord’s The Society of the Spectacle was his idea that we are increasingly experiencing life through images and representations instead of directly experiencing things ourselves. This made me think a lot about fashion and social media, especially Instagram. I sell vintage clothing, but I also photograph it, post it, style it, and think about how it will look on a screen. Sometimes I wonder if I am selling the actual garment or the image and feeling surrounding the garment. Something that is beautiful in person can look completely different in a photograph, and sometimes something that looks amazing online is actually disappointing in real life. The idea that the image can become more important than the thing itself felt very relevant to me. Debord describes the spectacle as more than just a collection of images, but as a way that our relationships become mediated through them, which made me think about how much of our lives now happens through screens. I also found the connection to “Welcome to Jankspace, Babes” really interesting because it feels like a very contemporary version of what Debord was talking about. The internet is now this strange, chaotic space filled with algorithms, AI slop, influencers, advertisements, trends, and endless images. I especially liked thinking about AI in relation to this because we are reaching a point where it can be difficult to tell what is real, what is manufactured, and what is made by a person. At the same time, I don’t think I completely dislike this spectacle. I actually love fashion, images, movies, and the weirdness of the internet. I think the part that makes me uncomfortable is when the representation becomes more important than the actual experience. It makes me wonder if we are becoming spectators of our own lives instead of actually living them.

Constituents of a theory of the media by Enzensberger (1970)

I always find it interesting to read people’s reactions because sometimes the comments completely change the way I see the original content. You find people who get it, people who see something you didn't, and people who completely kill the vibe lol. I find it funny to reconnect with this part of my internet history through the reading and realize that the internet wasn’t just a place where I consumed media. Theory: Chapter 1 & 2 from The Electronic Disturbance by CAE, 1994

October 27, 2026 One of the most interesting ideas I took away from The Electronic Disturbance, particularly the chapter “Nomadic Power and Cultural Resistance,” is that power does not always need to be physically present to influence our lives. The author compares the global expansion of capitalism to the historical spread of the Catholic Church, arguing that both create familiar spaces and symbols that make people feel connected and secure. His comparison between the Church's presence across different territories and the global presence of McDonald's made me think about how institutions establish power through familiarity. I also connected this idea to The Jetsons and The Simpsons, two animated shows that imagine or comment on technological futures. While The Jetsons presents futuristic technology as part of everyday life, The Simpsons often uses satire to expose problems in society. Looking at these cartoons today, I wonder whether they simply predicted certain technologies or whether they also revealed our existing hopes and fears about technological progress. Another point that stood out to me was the discussion of surveillance and the idea that power can operate without being easily located. Today, we are constantly interacting with screens, digital platforms, and systems that collect information about us, often without fully understanding how that information is used. I found it interesting to connect this to religion, where the idea of being watched by a higher power has historically influenced how people understand their actions and behavior. Although religious observation and digital surveillance are not the same, both raise questions about visibility, authority, and personal freedom. The reading left me thinking about whether technology genuinely gives us more freedom or simply creates new forms of control. I found the author's argument both unsettling and useful because it challenges us to look beyond technological innovation and question who benefits from it, who is being watched, and how we might resist these invisible forms of power.

Race After Technology by Ruha Benjamin (2019)

November 17, 2026 We tend to associate technology with progress, innovation, and making our lives easier, but we sometimes forget that behind these systems there are people making decisions, and those decisions can carry the same prejudices that already exist in society. Ruha Benjamin introduces the concept of the New Jim Code, which explains how racism and discrimination can continue through technology, even when these systems appear neutral. I also liked the reference to Frederick Douglass, who criticized those who revealed the routes enslaved people used to escape. There's something contradictory about the idea that sharing information is always a good thing, when sometimes keeping things secret is what allows people to survive or resist. Especially today, when almost everything we do online is being recorded, tracked, or collected in some way. Benjamin also talks about abolition, not simply as getting rid of harmful systems but imagining what could exist in their place. There's a comparison between wealthy neighborhoods like the Hamptons or the Upper West Side and poorer communities that says so much about how differently people experience freedom. Wealthy, predominantly white neighborhoods already enjoy something resembling abolition, where residents don't have to deal with constant police presence, surveillance, or being treated like criminals. Meanwhile, in other neighborhoods, police are everywhere. What's considered a radical idea for some people is already an everyday reality for others. It's strange how we can live in the same city and experience completely different versions of freedom. And then there's the question of technology itself. We have all these systems promising efficiency, security, and convenience, but who are they actually benefiting? Technology can make our lives easier while simultaneously making it easier to monitor and control people. I don't necessarily think technology itself is the problem, but rather how it's designed, who controls it, and whose interests it serves. Especially now that I'm learning to create websites and interactive projects, I wonder how much responsibility we have as creators to consider the people who will interact with what we make.

Introduction & GLITCH IS ERROR from Glith Feminism by Legacy Russel (2020)

November 24, 2026 Legacy starts the introduction with the word “tweenager,” which I had to look up. It's funny because I also started hanging out online when I was a tweenager, around the ages of eleven or twelve, although I had already been spending time on the internet since I was nine, mostly on YouTube and playing My Scene and Barbie computer games. When she mentioned Boricua culture being part of the bedrock of the East Village and the Lower East Side, it felt kind of surreal to me. I've never associated the East Village with Puerto Rican culture because of how predominantly white it feels now. It's one of the neighborhoods I frequent the most for a bowl of borscht, pierogies, Casey Rubber Stamps, and some other random stationery stores and dessert spots. It's kind of crazy to think that the East Village had such a strong Black and Puerto Rican presence not too long ago, especially considering that Legacy is only around forty years old. It puts into perspective how rapidly these neighborhoods have been gentrified. This particular part of the introduction felt very personal to me as someone who currently lives in a neighborhood that used to have a strong Puerto Rican presence (thank God there are still some of us left). Sometimes my white neighbors assume I'm a nanny or make me feel like I don't belong here, when my people were here before all of this gentrification. It makes me feel some sort of way and makes me want to stay and make this place Puerto Rican again! When Legacy talks about her twelve-year-old self becoming sixteen, twenty, and seventy, I used to do exactly the same thing. I would constantly change my age, ethnicity, and username to keep the fun and anonymity of being online. I was Alina, I was Valentina, I was Chloe, I was Saku, I was Satsuki, and many, many others. I spent so much time exchanging thoughts and experiences with faceless strangers, some of whom eventually had faces and became long-term friends and lovers. “Searching for those sites of experimenting where I could explore my true self. Open and ready to be read by those who spoke my language.” I love this because, in many ways, I became the person I am today because of all these strangers I met online, but most importantly because I had no filter. I wasn't afraid of being judged by someone who was miles away from me. Being ready to be read by those who spoke my language is so real because I could finally connect with people in a way I couldn't with those around me at school or in my neighborhood. I was just a random Puerto Rican girl from Humacao with a small world but a very large imagination, always craving to know more about the rest of the world. There's something almost contradictory about the vulnerability of being online. You can choose to be yourself or someone completely different, yet sometimes pretending to be someone else allows you to be more yourself. Unlike AFK (away from keyboard), where there are certain limitations because of social consequences, embarrassment, or expectations, the internet gave me more freedom to experiment. A block button doesn't really work the same way AFK as it does online. Legacy's discussion of existing within a binary system, where we are expected to assume that our identities are unchangeable and that how we are read by the world is chosen for us, also explains why the internet felt so freeing to me. AFK, I didn't always feel like I could change, be different, or simply be weird. I always knew I wanted to be an artist growing up, but I also wasn't entirely sure how to become one. My world was small, and I was constantly trying to expand it through these chats. Eventually, though, I started questioning how much time I was spending online. I wanted to experience things AFK and have more face-to-face connections. I couldn't find a healthy balance between these two worlds, so I somewhat disconnected myself from the internet and started “living life.” I had a similar existential crisis with filmmaking. I remember questioning why I was spending so much time writing stories when I could actually go out into the world and live them. And I did! I traveled, met people, and experienced a bunch of things. Now I feel ready to return to making art while maintaining a healthier relationship with these two worlds. I don't know why I was so existential about making art versus living life when I could have been doing both, but I'm working on it now. Maybe that was my own glitch: thinking that I had to choose between the two, or being insecure about making art because I thought I needed a lot of money to make something worthwhile. We are so conditioned to understand errors as something that needs to be immediately corrected, like when there's a glitch on Instagram and everyone freaks out. What I like about Russell's proposition in Glitch Is Error is that the glitch doesn't necessarily need to be fixed. It can be a disruption that allows us to question why something is supposed to function a certain way in the first place. I also wanted to mention the statement Russell includes about reparations for Black Americans. It made me think about Indigenous Americans as well, and how little accountability there has been for the atrocities committed against the Indigenous people of this land and the Africans who were forcibly brought here to be exploited. Especially considering that Thanksgiving is this week and how normalized it is to celebrate it without questioning its history.
